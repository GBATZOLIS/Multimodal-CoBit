\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Multimodal generative models must reconcile data with very different structures. Images and audio
are commonly modelled through continuous diffusion, whereas language and protein sequences are
usually generated autoregressively or with discrete corruption processes
\citep{ho2020denoising,song2021score,austin2021structured,sahoo2024simple,wang2024dplm}. Existing
systems bridge these choices through modality-specific encoders, latent spaces, objectives or
prediction interfaces \citep{bao2023unidiffuser,tang2023codi,campbell2024multiflow,wang2025dplm2}.
These designs are effective, but each new modality pairing introduces another interface to design
and calibrate.

In this work, we ask whether one denoising interface and objective can instead model joint distributions over discrete codes from very different modality pairs, with domain-specific choices confined to tokenizers, layouts and inference settings. Bit sequences are the fundamental language of digital computers: a fixed binary code needs no learned embedding and its width grows only logarithmically with the vocabulary, so codes from different vocabularies can share one input projection and one prediction head. Our starting point is therefore continuous bitstream diffusion \citep{method:cobit}, which writes discrete codes as bits and applies continuous Gaussian diffusion to them. Each modality is written as bits, directly or through a frozen, domain-specific tokenizer, placed in a shared binary vocabulary and arranged in a fixed sequence template. Inside the denoiser, every position uses the same input
projection, transformer backbone, bitwise prediction head and loss. A partial-denoising objective
then trains one checkpoint to perform joint generation and every conditional direction included in
training: clean positions specify the conditioning signal, and corrupted positions specify what to
generate. To test whether the denoiser itself needs modality-specific components, the architecture contains no modality- or segment-specific parameters.

We test this question in three settings that differ in the kind of cross-modal dependence they contain. MNIST-Sum pairs raw binary pixels with symbolic equations and uses no learned tokenizer, so it isolates whether visual and symbolic bits form one learnable joint distribution. Speech and text are strongly asymmetric: speech carries acoustic and speaker information absent from the transcript and reaches the model through a lossy codec. Protein sequence and structure are aligned residue by residue and physically coupled rather than semantically related. The components are those of CoBit; our contribution is the evidence that one denoising formulation carries across these three kinds of dependence.
