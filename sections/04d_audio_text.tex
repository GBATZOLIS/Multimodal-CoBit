% Paragraph 1: Speech-specific setup: tokenization with StableCodec + Speaker Conditioning

We tokenize speech with StableCodec \citep{audio:stablecodec}, which performs FSQ \citep{audio:fsq} on the latent space of a transformer autoencoder. The resulting sequences represent speech at a fixed token rate of 25 tokens per second from a vocabulary of size 46,656. While RVQ \citep{audio:soundstream} is the predominant choice for speech tokenization, it would be problematic in the diffusion setup: flattening the codebooks like Llama-Mimi \citep{audio:llama-mimi} would introduce strict positional dependencies and major computational overhead (bidirectional attention scales quadratically with sequence length); treating the Cartesian product of codebooks as a single vocabulary would neglect their conditional structure. FSQ-based tokenization is hence more suitable for bitstream diffusion, but StableCodec does not explicitly model speaker identity. To control voice characteristics, we augment the input sequence with speaker tokens. BiCodec-global \citep{audio:spark-tts} performs FSQ on the outputs of an ECAPA-TDNN \citep{audio:ecapa-tdnn} speaker verification model with a vocabulary of 4,096 entries to produce a fixed-length sequence, independent of the input duration. Speaker tokens are not used during waveform decoding; they are included purely as a conditioning signal for generation. Combining these with the text vocabulary used for image-text experiments (\ref{sec:exp:imagetext}) results in a vocabulary of 250,772 tokens (with separate pad tokens for text and speech) and a sequence of 632 tokens in total: 100 text tokens, 32 speaker tokens, 500 speech tokens.

% Paragraph 2: Speech-native tasks: emphasis on continuation rather than joint generation (+ training/test sets)

Four speech-native tasks are considered: unconditional co-generation, speech continuation, zero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) and automatic speech recognition (ASR). In co-generation, the entire sequence is generated from scratch; in speech continuation, speaker and speech tokens are extracted from the start of the audio (typically the first 3 seconds). For ASR, the complete speaker and speech sequences are used as input; for TTS, text tokens and BiCodec-global tokens from a held-out recording of the target speaker are provided. Additional tasks that fit the text-speaker-speech structure can be supported simply by modifying the training recipe (\ref{sec:method:directions}). The model is trained on 46k hours of LibriTTS \citep{audio:libritts} and MLS-English \citep{audio:mls} data, divided in 11.8 million sequences that contain single-speaker, reading-style paired text-audio samples derived from the LibriVox audiobook corpus. Equal training effort is devoted to each of the four tasks. Evaluation is conducted on the LibriSpeech-PC \citep{audio:librispeech-pc} test partitions and the SALMon \citep{audio:salmon} benchmark. The final sampler configurations (stochasticity and guidance scale) for each conditional task have been selected based on modality alignment (WER) on 256 samples from the LibriTTS dev-clean set.

% Paragraph 3: Joint Generation

Autoregressive Speech Language Models (SLMs) are typically not evaluated on paired unconditional generation. They either cannot produce both modalities simultaneously or cannot operate without any input. Instead, we compare CoBit's co-generation abilities to an autoregressive system that generates one modality at a time. Results are provided in Table \ref{tab:app:audio:co-generation}, and more details about the cascaded pipeline can be found in Appendix \ref{app:audio:exp:baselines}. CoBit produces strongly aligned text and audio with very few conflicts at the character and word levels, matching the text-LM+TTS combination of the cascade. In terms of perplexity under an external LM, the content remains linguistically plausible although scoring worse than the text-LM-based generation. The disparity between text and speech perplexities for CoBit can largely be attributed to the \texttt{Whisper-large} model used to transcribe the audio, which normalizes its outputs into well-structured sentences. Notably, the model generates natural-sounding speech even when sampling the speaker-conditioning tokens from scratch. While the cascade system achieves a higher UTMOS score, it does so by conditioning the speech decoder on BiCodec-global tokens, strictly forcing voice and prosody preservation throughout the audio.

% Paragraph 4: Continuation - semantic/acoustic quality (LibriSpeech evaluation)

Speech continuation is the main generative evaluation axis for autoregressive SLMs. Given a short audio prompt, models are tasked with producing continuations that match both the acoustic setting and the semantic content. Table \ref{tab:app:audio:continuation} reports evaluation results on LibriSpeech-PC test-clean. The model consistently achieves the second best score in all aspects except LLM-as-a-Judge, ranking only behind TASLM-Embed \citep{audio:taste}. CoBit's continuations are linguistically plausible, achieving very low perplexity and comfortably outperforming models with over a billion parameters. While constructing well-formed sentences, most SLMs are incapable of producing relevant continuations to the prompt's content. The same failure is observed for CoBit, which performs moderately worse than almost all autoregressive models that operate on fixed-rate speech tokenization, but differ in scale and text pre-training \citep{audio:twist}. Even warm initialization, however, appears to be insufficient for semantic extrapolation; thematically accurate speech is only achieved by TASLM-Embed, which uses text-aligned speech representations that close the semantic gap between text and speech modelling. Reinforcing the co-generation results, CoBit generates decisively more natural audio than all models that do not condition the decoder on speaker-related information. It is also able to match distributional characteristics of the ground-truth continuations in terms of frame-level details and expressiveness better than all such models, outperforming them in FSD metrics by a wide margin. This can be credited to classifier-free guidance, which steers generation towards the mode of the prompt-conditional distribution.

% Paragraph 5: Continuation - SALMon

Generating speech requires capturing multiple, often unrelated aspects that form the linguistic and acoustic content, such as syntax or background noise. For autoregressive models, such properties are evaluated in isolation using likelihood-based protocols \citep{audio:twist, audio:salmon, audio:swuggy-sblimp}: the SLM is provided with the true sample and an alternative version where the property of choice is modified, and is tasked with assigning a higher likelihood to the positive example. However, the likelihood estimation capabilities of diffusion models are not representative of their generative capacity \citep{audio:diffusion-likelihood}. Instead, we adopt the recently proposed generation-based version of the SALMON benchmark \citep{audio:gen-salmon} to assess CoBit's ability to capture acoustic details (see Appendix \ref{app:audio:exp:eval} for more details). Table \ref{tab:app:audio:salmon} presents the per-task scores. On average, CoBit outperforms all other text-speech hybrids and the Flow-SLM-S \citep{audio:flow-slm} variant, scoring below larger speech-only models with over 1B parameters. For voice-related tasks (sentiment, speaker, gender), CoBit is on par with the strongest baselines, with the aid of the BiCodec-global features that capture such characteristics. In noise-related tasks (background and room), the model sits in between the two clusters of baselines: it does better than the rest of the multimodal SLMs that focus heavily on speech semantics, yet it cannot match the speech-only baselines that use the high-bandwidth Mimi tokenizer \citep{audio:moshi}, which is trained on a larger and more diverse corpus than StableCodec and can thus capture more fine-grained details in the audio signal that are not necessarily related to the actual speech.

% Paragraph 6: Cross-modal transfer (Text-to-speech and speech-to-text)

Partial denoising enables straightforward text-to-speech and speech-to-text transfer for CoBit; this is not the case for other SLMs, which require task-specific fine-tuning or engineering adjustments like few-shot prompting and time-delayed streaming generation (see Appendix \ref{app:audio:exp:baselines} for an overview). TTS and ASR performance is outlined in Table \ref{tab:app:audio:cross-modal}. As already observed for co-generation, bitstream diffusion produced very few errors in text and speech alignment. For TTS in particular, CoBit is competitive with SLMs with up to 7B parameters: it surpasses SpiritLM \citep{audio:spirit-lm} on CER ($4.1\%$ compared to $6.7\%$) and approaches Moshi \citep{audio:moshi} on WER ($5.3\%$ compared to $4.7\%$). While the generated speech remains natural, it cannot exactly reconstruct the required speaker characteristics: the BiCodec-global conditioning steers the speech tokens towards the provided voice, but their absence from the decoder prevents an exact speaker match. The contrast in SpkSim with GPA-v1.5 \citep{audio:gpa}, which has a similar scale at 505M parameters and conditions its vocoder to the same BiCodec-global tokens, further verifies this finding. Despite its TTS success, CoBit cannot transcribe speech as accurately as text-initialized systems. This is expected when looking at the design of ASR systems: they typically recognize speech at the character or phoneme level using self-supervised representations, and then employ a text LM to decode their output into well-structured sentences \citep{audio:whisper}. For SLMs, the textually pre-trained backbone implicitly plays a similar role, leading to more coherent transcriptions, as previously demonstrated \citep{audio:voxtlm}.

% Paragraph 7: Text pre-training + Text-Audio conclusion

The multimodal CoBit framework consistently showcases strong performance across all text-audio tasks, balancing modality alignment, linguistic fluency and acoustic naturalness. Notably, it is also constitutes the first fully non-autoregressive SLM, as all other architectures generate autoregressively and strictly non-autoregressive systems are restricted to ASR-TTS hybrids. Its core weaknesses (ASR, thematic preservation and exact voice reconstruction) can be tied to two design choices that baselines consistently exploit: initialization from a text pre-trained transformer with the substitution of its input and output layers and explicit speaker conditioning on the decoder. CoBit is instead trained from scratch on paired data, and waveforms are generated exclusively from StableCodec tokens. These are deliberate choices: the aim of this work is to show that continuous bitstream diffusion has strong multimodal learning capabilities without modality-specific adjustments. Both modifications are straightforward in principle: warm-initializing CoBit from a pre-trained text checkpoint mainly required adapting the components that are tied to the sequence length and the vocabulary bit-width; speaker conditioning for the decoder is simply a matter of fine-tuning on the joint distribution of BiCodec-global and StableCodec tokens. We expect both changes to close the remaining performance gap and leave their implementation to a dedicated line of work on text-audio CoBit.
